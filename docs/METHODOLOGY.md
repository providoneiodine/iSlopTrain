# iSlopTrain Vision: training methodology

The target behaviour: **eyes as far down the track as possible, moving to the apex of the next corner as early as possible, without conscious effort.** This document explains why each drill is built the way it is.

> References were compiled from the literature as known at the time of writing and have not been re-checked line by line. Verify a citation before quoting it externally.

## 1. What expert drivers actually do with their eyes

| Finding | Source | Used in |
|---|---|---|
| Drivers look at the inside of an upcoming bend (the *tangent point*) 1–2 s before entering it. Gaze leads steering by ~1 s. | Land & Lee (1994), *Nature* 369, 742–744 | Apex Snap target placement & timing window |
| A racing driver aimed gaze, helped by head rotation, at the apex region well before turn-in, very consistently lap to lap. | Land & Tatler (2001), *Current Biology* 11(15), 1215–1220 | Apex Snap |
| Steering uses a **far point** (anticipation) and a **near point** (lane position), the near point mostly picked up peripherally. | Land & Horwood (1995), *Nature* 377, 339–340; Salvucci & Gray (2004), *Perception* 33(10), 1233–1248 | Two-Point Focus, Apex Snap marshal lights |
| Lane keeping works well on peripheral vision alone. | Summala, Nieminen & Punto (1996), *Human Factors* 38(3), 442–451 | Two-Point Focus |
| Look-ahead fixations beyond the current curve are part of skilled curve driving and more frequent with experience. | Lehtonen et al. (2014), *Accident Analysis & Prevention* 70, 195–208; Lappi (2014), *J. Vision* 14(12):21 | Apex Snap "next apex" rule |
| Racing drivers differ from non-racing drivers in gaze behaviour in a simulator. | van Leeuwen et al. (2017), *PLoS ONE* 12(11): e0186871 | Rationale for task-specific training |

**Design consequence:** the drills train one consistent rule: *"clip the apex → eyes to the next apex"*, with the near field handled by peripheral vision.

## 2. Making it a reflex rather than a decision

### Consistent mapping → automaticity
Responses become automatic (fast, effortless, resistant to distraction and load) through many repetitions of a **consistent** stimulus–response mapping (Schneider & Shiffrin, 1977, *Psych. Review* 84(1)). In Apex Snap the cue is always the same (passing an apex) and so is the correct response (move gaze to the next apex). Every lap gives dozens of identical repetitions.

### Anticipation, not reaction
Orienting can be *exogenous* (pulled by a sudden event) or *endogenous* (directed in advance) (Posner, 1980, *Q. J. Exp. Psychol.* 32). We want the endogenous kind. Three mechanisms enforce it:

1. **Brief masked flashes.** The target board always shows a pattern mask, a superimposition of all four arrows (Breitmeyer & Ögmen, 2006, *Visual Masking*). The arrow replaces it for a brief, adaptive time and then the mask returns. Saccade latency is typically ~200 ms, and noticing, moving the eyes and reading the target takes longer. Once the flash is shorter than that, **only gaze already on the board succeeds**.
2. **Low-transient onset.** Because the mask contains every feature of the target, the flash causes almost no change in luminance or shape that would pull the eyes reflexively.
3. **Uniform timing across the window.** The flash can appear at any moment between passing the previous apex and turn-in of the next corner. Arriving late with your eyes means missing early flashes. **Snap score** (accuracy on the earliest third) measures exactly this.

### Challenge point and adaptive difficulty
Learning is greatest when the task is neither trivial nor overwhelming (Guadagnoli & Lee, 2004, *J. Motor Behavior* 36(2)). All timed drills use a 2-down/1-up transformed staircase (Levitt, 1971, *JASA* 49) on flash duration. It converges on ~70.7 % correct and keeps every session at the edge of your ability. Thresholds carry over between sessions (starting at 1.5× last threshold).

### Feedback that fades
Feedback on every trial speeds early acquisition but can create dependence. Reduced-frequency or summary feedback gives better retention (Winstein & Schmidt, 1990, *JEP:LMC* 16(4); Salmoni, Schmidt & Walter, 1984, *Psych. Bull.* 95(3)). Each drill offers *every trial* or *summary only*. Start with every trial and move to summary-only once you are consistent.

### Variability
Each run generates a new procedural circuit, and variable practice supports generalisation to circuits you have not seen. What varies:
- **Corner vocabulary:** kinks, sweepers, medium and tight corners, hairpins, decreasing-radius corners (late apex), increasing-radius corners (early apex), plus compound features: chicanes (2–3 flicks), esses (3–4 alternating bends) and double-apex corners.
- **Track** setting (Mixed / Technical / Fast) weights the vocabulary. Technical tracks pack apexes closely, so the "apex → next apex" flick has to happen in well under a second, as through a chicane in a real car.
- **Elevation** (Flat / Rolling / Hilly): uphill and downhill sections, crests and compressions. Blind crests hide the next apex until you crest, a real look-ahead problem where the eyes must go to where the apex *will* appear. Gravity also changes braking and acceleration, so look-ahead time changes with gradient.
- **Speed and look-ahead time:** these follow from the car's grip, braking and acceleration limits applied to each layout.

## 3. Attention width: countering tunnel vision
High arousal narrows the range of cues used (Easterbrook, 1959, *Psych. Review* 66(3)). That is the "tunnel vision" drivers report in races. Useful-field-of-view (UFOV) training pairs central identification with peripheral localisation under brief masked exposure (Ball & Owsley, 1993). In older drivers it has been associated with fewer at-fault crashes (Ball, Edwards, Ross & McGwin, 2010, *J. Am. Geriatr. Soc.* 58(11)). **Two-Point Focus** adapts the format to racing geometry: central = far apex, peripheral = near-field flag. **Apex Snap**'s optional marshal lights add the same demand while moving.

## 3b. Keeping the habit under race load (Race Mode)
A habit trained in a clean environment can break when the race gets busy. **Apex Snap: Race Mode** adds the information competing for a racer's eyes: cars on the line ahead (brake lights), a relative timing box, shift lights, delta and warnings on the dash, engineer radio and spotter calls, with optional engine and speech audio.
- **Representative learning design:** practice should keep competition's information sources so perception and action stay coupled (Pinder, Davids, Renshaw & Araújo, 2011, *JSEP* 33(1)).
- **Attentional Control Theory:** anxiety and load bias attention toward salient, stimulus-driven distractors at the cost of goal-directed control (Eysenck, Derakshan, Santos & Calvo, 2007, *Emotion* 7(2)). Practising the goal-directed gaze habit amid salient clutter targets exactly this.
- **Training with anxiety/load transfers to pressure:** Oudejans & Pijpers (2009), *QJEP* 62(8).
- **Go/no-go:** Confirm is pressed only for threats (car alongside, marshal light) and never for radio or dash noise, which trains inhibition of capture (cf. Verbruggen & Logan, 2008, *TiCS* 12(11)). Threat cues are deliberately redundant and peripheral or auditory (red relative row, spotter call, car nose at the screen edge), so they can be caught without moving the eyes off the apex.
- **Brief, well-timed HUD glances:** long glances away from the road ahead sharply raise crash risk (Klauer et al., 2006, NHTSA 100-Car Study). Coaching pushes "one quick relative check on straights".
- **Load cost** = Race Mode flash threshold − latest standard Apex Snap threshold. It measures how much the clutter disrupts the habit. A shrinking load cost is the sign that the habit is becoming automatic, because automatic processes are resistant to concurrent load (Schneider & Shiffrin, 1977).

## 3c. Holding the apex under threat (Eyes Off the Wall, Mind tab)
Drivers and riders call it target fixation: you look at the wall, the gravel or the stricken car and drive towards it. Motorcycle coaching treats it as a "survival reaction" to be trained out (Code, 1993, *A Twist of the Wrist II*). The research explains why it happens and why it gets worse under pressure:

| Finding | Source | Used in |
|---|---|---|
| Sudden new objects capture the eyes even when they are irrelevant, often without awareness. | Theeuwes, Kramer, Hahn & Irwin (1998), *Psychological Science* 9(5) | Sudden hazards timed just before the flash |
| Anxiety shifts attention from goal-directed to stimulus-driven control, towards threats. | Eysenck, Derakshan, Santos & Calvo (2007), *Emotion* 7(2) | Threat on the outside, goal on the inside |
| Anxious performers shorten their final fixation on the target. | Wilson, Vine & Wood (2009), *JSEP* 31(2) | Masked flash rewards a held fixation |
| "Don't overshoot" under load produced overshooting. | Wegner, Ansfield & Pilloff (1998), *Psychological Science* 9(3) | Self-talk compare mode |
| Told to avoid the keeper, penalty takers' ironic errors (shots near the keeper) came with shorter final fixations on the open goal. | Binsch, Oudejans, Bakker & Savelsbergh (2010), *Human Movement Science* 29(2) | Self-talk compare mode |
| Naming the thing to avoid drives the effect, not only the word "don't", and gaze mediates it. | Binsch, Oudejans, Bakker & Savelsbergh (2010), *Int. J. Sport Psychol.* | IRONIC tip ("say what to do") |
| Training under pressure improves performance under pressure (meta-analysis, g = 0.67). | Low, Sandercock, Freeman, Winter, Butt & Maynard (2021), *Sport, Exercise, and Performance Psychology* | Adaptive threat level |

**How the drill measures where your eyes were.** On hazard corners a barrier stands on the outside between turn-in and the apex. It carries a yellow-bordered board with the same pattern mask as the apex board. On probe trials both boards flash a *different* arrow for the same brief, masked exposure. Because the flash is shorter than an eye movement, you can only read the board you were already looking at, so the arrow you report shows where your eyes were. Probes count only when the two boards are well apart on screen (at least 7 % of the width and 3 board widths).

- **Eyes captured** (primary) = hazard-arrow answers on probes, corrected for guessing. A guess is as likely to land on the hazard arrow as on each of the two other wrong arrows, so half of the wrong-arrow answers are subtracted. The raw rate is stored as `captureRaw`.
- **Threat cost** = apex accuracy on clean corners minus apex accuracy on hazard corners. Clean and hazard corners are interleaved on the same staircase, so they are compared at the same flash durations.
- **Threat level** (1-6) sets the share of corners with hazards (35-80 % nominal, a bit less after skipping chicanes where the next apex board would sit beside the barrier), which hazard types appear, how often they appear suddenly, how early before the flash (1.2 s down to 0.1 s), how close the barrier is to the edge (5 m down to ~1 m), and impact sound at 5+. With **Auto**, the level goes up after a session with ≤12 % capture and ≤8 pts threat cost, and down after ≥30 % capture or ≥25 pts threat cost (at least 8 probes). This is graded exposure, as in stress inoculation, rather than a single high-stakes test.
- **Self-talk compare** alternates "Eyes: apex" and "Don't look at the wall" every 40 s. The **"Don't look" effect** is capture under the avoidance cue minus capture under the approach cue: your own evidence for which cue to use in the car.

**Limits.** Hazards in a pseudo-3D drill are not a real wall at 200 km/h, and the drill can't produce real fear. What it trains is the attentional habit (goal-directed gaze despite a salient, threat-coded distractor) and the choice of self-talk. Check transfer in the sim at walled corners.

## 3d. Recovering from a run of errors (Clean Slate, Mind tab)
One mistake rarely costs a race. The second and third do: the tentative lap after an off, or the lunge to win the time back. Clean Slate trains the moment after the mistake, using an abstract task instead of a staged race incident, because a fake punt doesn't make anyone genuinely angry. The errors here are real: you are pushed past your limit and you fail.

| Finding | Source | Used in |
|---|---|---|
| People slow down after errors, and the next answer is often less accurate, not more. | Rabbitt (1966), *J. Exp. Psychol.* 71(2); Notebaert et al. (2009), *Cognition* 111(2) | Post-error shift, accuracy after an error |
| Post-error slowing is confounded by slow drifts in speed; compare the trials just before and just after each error. | Dutilh et al. (2012), *J. Math. Psychol.* 56(3) | Post-error shift (robust method) |
| Flanker arrows measure how well conflicting information is ignored. | Eriksen & Eriksen (1974), *Perception & Psychophysics* 16(1) | Grey arrows beside the blue one |
| Switching between task rules carries a reliable cost. | Monsell (2003), *Trends Cogn. Sci.* 7(3) | Orange rows (press the opposite way), Flip bursts |
| A weighted up/down staircase converges on any target accuracy. | Kaernbach (1991), *Perception & Psychophysics* 49(3) | Reveal lead held at ~88 % correct |
| Anxiety shifts attention towards irrelevant, salient and threatening stimuli. | Eysenck et al. (2007), *Emotion* 7(2) | Distraction ladder |
| Entirely irrelevant distractors still cost performance. | Forster & Lavie (2008), *J. Exp. Psychol. Applied* 14(1) | Distraction ladder |
| Training under mild anxiety protects against choking at higher anxiety; pressure training transfers (g ≈ 0.67). | Oudejans & Pijpers (2010), *Psychol. Sport Exerc.* 11(1); Low et al. (2021) | Burst ladder, compounding penalty |

**Task.** It plays like a rhythm game. Rows of five arrows scroll down a lane and reach the hit line every 850 ms. Each row shows grey pattern masks (all four arrows superimposed) until it crosses the reveal line, then turns into arrows. One arrow, in a random slot, is coloured: **blue** means press its direction, **orange** (15 % of normal rows) means press the opposite direction. The grey arrows point the same way as the coloured one or not (half each). Press as the row crosses the hit window, ±150 ms around the line. Pressing after the reveal but before the window is an **early** error, a wrong direction is **wrong**, and letting the row pass is a **miss**. Correct presses are graded PERFECT (≤45 ms from the line), GREAT (≤95 ms) or GOOD.

The rows keep a steady beat, so timing is anticipated, not reacted to. Reading is the reacted part: the staircase moves the **reveal lead** (how long before the line the arrows appear) on normal rows only. It descends quickly until the first error, then takes a weighted walk (error: ×1.15 then ×1.08; correct: ÷ the same step to the power 0.12/0.88) that holds about 88 % correct. Reaction time is measured from the reveal.

**Bursts** come without warning after a 12-row warm-up. During a burst the reveal lead is frozen at its pre-burst value and then:
- **Squeeze:** reveal cut to 60 % (level 1) down to 48 % (level 6) of the lead.
- **Rush** (level 2+): the beat drops to 480 ms, so rows come almost twice as fast, at 85 % of the lead.
- **Clutter** (level 3+): seven arrows, all grey ones against the coloured one, scattered, with a blue-grey lookalike and visual noise, at 85 % of the lead.
- **Flip** (level 4+): 65 % of rows are orange, at 90 % of the lead.
- **Double hit** (level 4+): a second burst starts 2-4 rows into the recovery from the first. The first burst's recovery is not scored; the second's is.

For the 10 rows after a burst the beat is normal and the reveal lead stays at its pre-burst value, so any dip is yours, not the task's. Burst level 1-6 sets burst length (3 up to 5-7 rows), the gap between bursts (24-32 down to 15-22 rows), the types and the double-hit chance (0 / 20 / 35 / 50 %).

**Metrics.**
- **Recovery** (primary) = the number of post-burst rows before the first run of 3 clean answers (correct and no slower than 1.25× baseline median from reveal). 0 = straight back. Never recovered within 10 rows scores 10.
- **Recovery cost** = baseline accuracy minus accuracy over the 10 post-burst rows. **Post-burst slowdown** = their median correct time vs baseline.
- **Error chaining** = P(error | previous error) − P(error | previous correct), consecutive normal rows only.
- **Post-error shift** = mean of RT(n+1) − RT(n−1) around errors with correct neighbours (Dutilh et al., 2012). Because presses land in the hit window, a positive shift means pressing later in the window. Labelled **hesitant** at ≥ +40 ms, **rushing** at ≤ −25 ms or when accuracy after an error falls 12+ pts below baseline without slowing, otherwise **steady**.
- **Perfect hits** and **timing error** (mean distance from the line on correct presses) track rhythm.
- **Recovery curves** on the Stats page pool accuracy and relative speed at each of the 8 post-burst positions: your last 5 sessions against the 10 before them. Progress shows as a curve that gets flatter and shorter.

**Distraction ladder** (0-6) puts distractors on 25-50 % of normal rows, timed from 200 ms before to 150 ms after the reveal: peripheral flashes (1+), taunt words such as "WRONG" or "TOO LATE" (2+), decoy arrows outside the lane, some of them blue (3+), chirps and a row that drifts sideways after the reveal (4+), a fake error buzz on a row you haven't answered (5+), a HUD glitch that makes the chain look broken, and two at once (6). Distractors never cover the hit zone, and bursts never carry them. **Distraction cost** = accuracy on distraction-free normal rows minus accuracy with distractors.

**Auto levels.** Burst level goes up after ≤1.5 rows recovery and ≤10 pts recovery cost, and down after ≥4 rows or ≥30 pts (at least 3 measured bursts). Distraction level goes up after ≤4 pts and ≤30 ms cost, and down after ≥15 pts or ≥90 ms (at least 15 distractor rows).

**Chain.** Correct answers build a chain with multiplier tiers at 5, 10, 20, 35, 50, 75 and 100 (×2 up to ×10), timing grades (PERFECT +10, GREAT +5 before the multiplier), rising tones, a hit-line flash, tier fanfares and a best-chain record. Errors in a row cost 20, 40, 80, then 160 points each (the cap keeps a bad burst from wiping out a session's chain points). Recovering after a burst pays a bonus that shrinks with every row it takes. The chain is there to make clean answers rewarding enough to balance the sting of errors, and the bonus rewards exactly the skill being measured.

**Reset cue** (optional): press Confirm the moment you consciously let a bad run go. The drill compares recovery with and without the press, as a first step towards a cue you can use in the car.

**Limits.** Post-error behaviour in a lab task isn't proven to carry over to driving. The yardstick is in the sim: compare the lap straight after a mistake with your average lap, before and after a few weeks of training.

## 4. Warm-up and readiness
Reaction-light drills are a staple of driver warm-ups and fitness programmes and part of a pre-performance routine (Cotterill, 2010, *Int. Rev. Sport Exerc. Psychol.* 3(2)). **Start Lights** uses a random hold so you cannot time the start from rhythm (Niemi & Näätänen, 1981, *Psych. Bull.* 89(1)). Responses under 100 ms count as anticipations, the same limit used in athletics false-start rules (Pain & Hibbs, 2007, *J. Sports Sci.* 25(1)). It also provides a daily readiness baseline: a much slower median than usual is a sign of fatigue.

## 5. Dose and scheduling
- **Short and frequent beats long and rare.** Distributed practice gives better retention than massed practice (Cepeda et al., 2006, *Psych. Bull.* 132(3)).
- Recommended: **5–10 min, 4–6 days/week**, ideally **immediately before driving** (the *Pre-drive* session), so the habit is primed when you need it.
- Blocks of ≤3 min with breaks limit visual fatigue. Stop if eyes feel strained.
- Use the **same screen, seat position and FOV** as your sim. Transfer is best when training conditions match performance conditions.

## 5b. Feedback from the Stats page
- **Trends, not single sessions:** notes compare your last block (up to 5 sessions) with the block before it and ignore changes under 3%. Session-to-session noise is normal, so you shouldn't react to one bad day.
- **Plateau detection:** a flat primary metric over ~10 sessions prompts you to raise difficulty, which keeps practice at the challenge point (Guadagnoli & Lee, 2004).
- **Weak-spot breakdowns** need at least 6 targets per bucket before they are judged. Smaller buckets are drawn faded.
- **In-sim transfer:** each weakness maps to a concrete in-sim exercise (e.g. say "next" at every apex, rehearse one chicane, learn crest references). This bridges the drill and real driving, because transfer is not automatic (Appelbaum & Erickson, 2018). Check the result on lap and sector times.

## 5c. Game feel (chains, scores, grades)
Every drill shares one game-feel layer (`components/juice.js`): a chain of correct answers with multiplier tiers at 5, 10, 20, 35, 50, 75 and 100, points with bonuses for harder work, rising tones, and "new best chain" / "PB pace" callouts. The results screen grades each session against your own history: **S** for a new personal best on the primary metric, then A / B / C by the share of your earlier sessions it beats, with record badges, a score that counts up, and a sheen and ring pulse on the grade for new PBs, best chains and high scores. The aim is to make you want to come back tomorrow, which is what spaced practice needs (section 5).

Rules that keep it from working against the training:
- **The bonuses reward the trained behaviour.** Apex Snap pays extra for early flashes ("SNAP!": eyes moved right after the apex) and for shorter flashes; Two-Point pays for shorter exposures and more distractors; Start Lights grades reaction time; Clean Slate rewards quick recovery. Guessing without a target breaks the chain.
- **Nothing covers what you have to look at.** The chain block sits in a corner chosen per drill (Apex Snap top-right, clear of the Race Mode dash, radio and relative; Two-Point top-left, outside the board and flag zones). Vision drills get no red edge flash and no screen shake, so the scene is never disturbed between flashes.
- **"Summary only" feedback stays summary only.** With that setting the chain is hidden and silent during the drill (Winstein & Schmidt, 1990); the score appears on the results screen.
- **Data → Game feel** switches between Full, Silent (no sounds) and Off.

## 6. Honest limits
- Generic "brain/vision training" transfers unevenly to on-field performance (Appelbaum & Erickson, 2018, *Int. Rev. Sport Exerc. Psychol.* 11(1)). These drills stay task-specific to give transfer the best chance, but **the real test is your lap times and consistency in the sim.** Track them.
- Without an eye tracker the app cannot see where you look. It infers gaze from what you could read. A good guesser can inflate scores slightly: 4-choice arrows give 25 % chance, and presses with no target are counted separately.
- Flash durations are quantised to display frames (16.7 ms at 60 Hz, 8.3 ms at 120 Hz). Compare thresholds on the same display.
- Pseudo-3D rendering approximates real perspective; very tight corners look more extreme than in a real sim.

## 7. Roadmap ideas (evidence-led)
- **Objective gaze:** optional webcam eye tracking (e.g. WebGazer.js) to measure gaze lead directly, or Tobii integration.
- **Head rotation:** Land & Tatler (2001) found head yaw leads into corners. A small local bridge from OpenTrack/SlimeVR (UDP → WebSocket) could train head lead as well.
- **Real circuits:** import track centre-lines from sim telemetry / track maps so the habit is rehearsed on the circuit you race next.
- **Imagery:** PETTLEP-structured lap visualisation (Holmes & Collins, 2001, *J. Appl. Sport Psychol.* 13(1)) with reference-point callouts.
- **Pressure training:** threat-under-load is covered by Eyes Off the Wall (section 3c). Still open: score stakes/time pressure (one-shot qualifying attempts), and a peripheral "call the hazard" response so hazards are acknowledged without being fixated.
